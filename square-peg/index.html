square peg pictures.

does every closed loop in the plane pass through the four corners of some square? nobody knows. these are pictures from working on it. each one says how much it establishes: proved, sketched, numerical, or retracted. so far every one is numerical, which means computed on polygons or sampled curves, not proved.

  1. numerical

    The chase, five rounds

    Start with a smooth oval, which has one inscribed square. At each round a log-spiral coil about a quarter of the square's side is put at a corner (the star) of the largest square, and the squares of the new smooth loop are found again. Each coloured outline is one square.

    The move does not kill squares. One coil turns 1 square into about 2,000, and after five coils about 10,000 were found. The largest square grows, from 0.74 to 0.90 of the loop's diameter. Most of the new squares have one corner on a strand of a coil and the others on the far sides of the loop: every strand of the coil gives its own slightly shifted copy of the old square.

    Limits: random starts, so counts are lower bounds; the parity check (the count should be odd) failed in two of six rounds, so squares were missed or counted twice; the squares drawn inside the coils use its open centre and inner connector and say nothing about a coil that winds all the way in. This tests one adversary's strategy, not the conjecture.

  2. numerical

    Squares on smooth two-arm coils, with an isolation test

    Twelve turns of two interleaved smooth arms (dark and light), three shapes. A square is counted only if it solves the equations to 12 digits, its corners are distinct, none is within 2% of an end of the coil, and it is isolated (its Jacobian is well conditioned). Random starts, so counts are lower bounds.

    r² falling linearly: none in this run. A longer run (1.9 million starts) found one large square using both arms, hit 10 times, and none with all corners on one arm, as Proposition 23 requires. Log spiral: none, in either run. Power law: about 1,500 here and about 20,000 in the longer run, on one arm and across both.

    The two controls behave: the affine arm alone has no square, and the power law has many. The phantom squares of the earlier polygon runs are gone. A double log spiral coil showing no squares away from its ends is what the project has believed since session 2; this is the first check of it with two arms and an isolation test.

  3. numerical

    Squares on the snowflake, and none on one side of it

    On a polygon a square with its corners on four given edges is one small linear solve, so every square can be listed. The closed snowflake has 12 at level 1 and 72 at level 2. One side of it alone, at level 3, has none (levels 1 and 2 also have none).

    The snowflake's squares use several sides at once. A small disc around a typical point sees one piece of one side, which seems to carry no squares. The failure mode is local; existence is global.

    Parallel-edge cases were skipped in the enumeration. The snowflake is calibration only: it is already covered by known theorems.

  4. numerical, partly retracted

    Rectangle size against diagonal angle

    Inscribed rectangles found by exact solves from random starts, on spiral loops of 3 to 24 turns and snowflakes of level 1 to 4. Horizontal axis: angle between the diagonals, 90° being a square. Vertical axis: size relative to the loop.

    What stands: large rectangles exist at every angle, and 90° looks no different from 85° or 75°.

    The search thins out on fine curves (snowflake level 4: two squares found), so counts are lower bounds.

    retracted: The small rectangles on the two spiral panels are withdrawn. On a polygonal spiral, tiny squares between neighbouring turns are artefacts of the polygon (the phantom squares of session 2). No isolation test was applied, so they prove nothing.

  5. numerical

    Half-squares at the centre of a double log spiral

    Rotate the loop a quarter turn about the starred point. Each crossing gives half a square with its right angle at the star; the dot is the forced fourth corner, blue if it lands inside the loop, red if outside.

    Double log spiral, 14 turns, star at the centre. No half-square uses the spiral's turns. The 201 crossings counted in the title all have size exactly 1: they lie on the outer closing arc, which is a circle about the star, and fall outside the frame.

    Pinning a corner at the wild point decides nothing: the earlier claim that such crossings always exist was wrong.
